***

## 1. Purpose

This document defines the Monte Carlo forecasting methodology used by DividendLab’s Simulation Engine in V1.  
It specifies how we model stochastic asset returns, income streams, and portfolio paths over time using probability theory and Monte Carlo simulation.  
The goal is to give developers a clear, implementable blueprint for generating simulated future outcomes (wealth and income distributions), and for interpreting those outputs in a way that is transparent and statistically defensible.

## 2. Executive Summary

Monte Carlo simulation is the core tool DividendLab uses to turn static portfolio characteristics (expected return, volatility, correlations, dividend behavior) into probabilistic forecasts of future wealth and income.  
Instead of a single deterministic projection, the engine generates many simulated paths of portfolio value and income, allowing us to estimate distributions, quantiles, confidence intervals, and downside risks.  

High level:

- Each asset’s total return is modeled as a random variable with specified distributional assumptions (e.g., normal or lognormal returns with given mean and volatility), plus a separate model for dividends and dividend growth.  
- The portfolio is evolved forward in discrete time steps (e.g., monthly or yearly) by applying simulated returns, contributions, withdrawals, and dividend flows, respecting portfolio weights and rebalancing rules defined in `portfolio_theory.md`. 
- The result is a large set of simulated paths from which we compute statistics such as median terminal wealth, probability of shortfall vs income goals, worst‑case drawdowns, and income distribution over time.  
- V1 deliberately uses simple, transparent models (e.g., geometric Brownian motion and/or historical bootstrapping) to avoid false precision and keep assumptions easy to explain; more sophisticated processes are deferred to later versions.  

This document explains what Monte Carlo simulation is, why DividendLab uses it, how it is implemented in V1, how it will be validated, and what its key limitations are.

## 3. Definitions

Core terms for the Monte Carlo engine:

- **Random Variable**  
  A quantity whose value is uncertain and described by a probability distribution (e.g., an asset’s one‑period return).

- **Probability Distribution**  
  A mathematical description of the likelihood of different outcomes (e.g., normal distribution for returns, lognormal for prices).

- **Drift (\(\mu\))**  
  The expected rate of return per unit time in the model; for GBM this is the deterministic trend component of log‑price.

- **Volatility (\(\sigma\))**  
  The standard deviation of returns per unit time; quantifies uncertainty around the drift.

- **Geometric Brownian Motion (GBM)**  
  A continuous‑time stochastic process commonly used to model asset prices, where log‑returns are normally distributed and prices remain positive.

- **Time Step / Horizon**  
  The discrete interval over which we simulate returns (e.g., monthly, yearly) and the total length of the simulation (e.g., 30 years).

- **Scenario / Path**  
  One full simulated trajectory of portfolio value and income over the forecast horizon.

- **Monte Carlo Simulation**  
  A method that uses repeated random sampling (many paths) to approximate the distribution of possible outcomes.

- **Confidence Interval**  
  A range of values (e.g., 5th–95th percentile of terminal wealth) that captures a specified probability mass of simulated outcomes.

- **Quantile**  
  A value below which a given percentage of simulated outcomes fall (e.g., 10th percentile income in year 20).

- **Correlation / Covariance Matrix**  
  Measures of co‑movement between asset returns; used to simulate multi‑asset portfolios with realistic joint behavior.

- **Income Path**  
  The sequence of dividend payments and withdrawals over time, as generated by the simulation.

- **Shortfall Risk**  
  Probability that income or wealth falls below a specified target at any point in the forecast.

These definitions must be used consistently across Monte Carlo, portfolio theory, validation, and stress testing so that all modules share a common language.

## 4. Core Concepts

### 4.1 Stochastic Return Modeling

- Asset returns are treated as random variables with specified distributions (e.g., normal or lognormal), parameterized by drift and volatility.  
- The simulation iteratively applies these random returns to current prices/values to generate future asset prices and portfolio values.

### 4.2 Path‑Dependent Behavior

- Portfolio outcomes depend on the entire sequence of returns, contributions, withdrawals, and dividend payments, not just on average returns.  
- Monte Carlo preserves this path dependency: sequence risk, drawdowns, and timing effects emerge naturally.

### 4.3 Portfolio‑Level Simulation

- Multi‑asset portfolios are simulated by drawing correlated returns from a joint distribution defined by the covariance matrix and portfolio weights.  
- Rebalancing, contributions, and withdrawals are applied each step according to rules specified in `portfolio_theory.md`, ensuring methodological consistency. 

### 4.4 Distribution‑Based Interpretation

- Rather than focusing on single point forecasts, we analyze distributions: median, quantiles, tails, and probabilities of hitting or missing targets.  
- Users see ranges of possible futures (e.g., income bands, wealth bands) and explicit downside probabilities, which supports more honest risk communication.

## 5. Mathematical Foundation

This section documents the key equations used in V1 Monte Carlo.

### 5.1 Single‑Asset GBM Model

For an asset \(i\), we model price \(S_t\) using geometric Brownian motion:

- **Continuous‑time representation (conceptual):**  
  \(\mathrm{d}S_t = \mu_i S_t \,\mathrm{d}t + \sigma_i S_t \,\mathrm{d}W_t\) 

- **Discrete‑time approximation (implementation):**  
  \[
  S_{t+\Delta t} = S_t \exp\left( \left(\mu_i - \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma_i^2\right)\Delta t + \sigma_i \sqrt{\Delta t}\,Z_{i,t} \right)
  \]    
  where \(Z_{i,t}\) is a standard normal random variable (possibly correlated across assets).

We document:

- Purpose: approximate realistic, positive asset prices over time.  
- Variables: \(S_t, \mu_i, \sigma_i, \Delta t, Z_{i,t}\).  
- Interpretation: drift is average trend; volatility controls randomness and tail risk.  
- Assumptions: constant drift/vol; log‑normal prices; no jumps; frictionless market.  
- Limitations: ignores fat tails, regime shifts, and jump risk (addressed in later versions).

### 5.2 Portfolio Return and Value Update

At each time step:

- Asset return: \(R_{i,t} = \frac{S_{i,t+\Delta t} - S_{i,t} + D_{i,t}}{S_{i,t}}\) 
- Portfolio value:  
  \[
  V_{t+\Delta t} = \left( V_t + C_t - W_t \right) \left( 1 + R_{p,t} \right)
  \]    
  where \(V_t\) is current value, \(C_t\) contributions, \(W_t\) withdrawals, and \(R_{p,t}\) is portfolio return computed from asset weights and correlated asset returns.

We specify:

- How weights are updated with rebalancing rules.  
- How dividends \(D_{i,t}\) are modeled (e.g., yield × price, with growth and possible cuts).  

### 5.3 Joint Distribution and Correlation

We model the vector of asset returns \(R_t\) as multivariate normal:

- \(R_t \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \Sigma)\)   
  where \(\mu\) is a vector of drifts and \(\Sigma\) is the covariance matrix.

Key points:

- Purpose: capture diversification and co‑movement.  
- Assumptions: linear correlation; constant covariance; normality in one‑period returns.  
- Implementation considerations: numerical stability of \(\Sigma\), regularization for small samples, and positive‑definiteness.

### 5.4 Confidence Intervals and Quantiles

From \(N\) simulated paths of a scalar outcome \(X\) (e.g., terminal wealth):

- Estimated \(p\)-th quantile: sort the sample and take the \(\lfloor pN \rfloor\)-th value.   
- Confidence band for time series (e.g., income): compute quantiles at each time step across paths.

We document:

- Intuition: more paths → more stable estimates.  
- Limitations: Monte Carlo error, dependence on model assumptions, and sensitivity to tails.

Whenever possible, we accompany equations with plots and numerical examples (e.g., simple two‑asset portfolio over 10 years) to build intuition for future developers.

## 6. Industry Standard Approaches

This section summarizes how professionals typically use Monte Carlo for portfolio and income forecasting. 

For each methodology, we document description, advantages, disadvantages, complexity, use cases, industry adoption, and suitability for DividendLab:

- **GBM‑Based Return Simulation**  
  - Description: classical lognormal price model with constant drift and volatility.  
  - Advantages: simple, tractable, widely documented.  
  - Disadvantages: poor tail modeling, ignores regime changes and jumps.  
  - Complexity: low.  
  - Use cases: baseline wealth projections, educational tools.  
  - Adoption: very common in retail tools and textbooks.  
  - Suitability: good for V1 as transparent baseline.

- **Historical Bootstrapping (IID Returns)**  
  - Description: resample historical returns to create pseudo‑future paths without parametric assumptions.  
  - Advantages: preserves empirical distribution, including some asymmetry and fat tails.  
  - Disadvantages: assumes future resembles past; limited regime modeling; sensitive to sample size.  
  - Complexity: low–medium.  
  - Use cases: quick stress tests and practical portfolio tools.  
  - Adoption: common in wealth management Monte Carlo engines.  
  - Suitability: strong candidate for V1/V2, especially for income scenario realism.

- **Block Bootstrapping / Regime‑Aware Bootstrapping**  
  - Description: resample blocks of consecutive returns or condition on regimes to preserve autocorrelation and volatility clustering.  
  - Advantages: better captures sequence risk and clustering of bad/good periods.  
  - Disadvantages: more complex, requires regime definition.  
  - Suitability: better for V2/V3.

- **Factor‑Based and Scenario‑Driven Models**  
  - Description: simulate macro factors or economic regimes, then derive asset returns from factor exposures.  
  - Advantages: interpretable scenarios (e.g., recession, inflation spike); better for stress testing.  
  - Disadvantages: much higher modeling complexity; heavy data requirements.  
  - Suitability: long‑term research and V3–V4.

## 7. DividendLab Approaches

We enumerate realistic implementation options for DividendLab and assign version recommendations. 

### Approach A: GBM with Constant Parameters (Baseline)

- Description: model each asset using GBM with constant drift/vol and a static covariance matrix.  
- Advantages: simple, transparent, analytically standard.  
- Disadvantages: weak tail modeling; limited regime capture.  
- Data Requirements: historical returns to estimate \(\mu\) and \(\Sigma\).  
- Computational Requirements: light; well within typical resource constraints.  
- Implementation Difficulty: low.  
- Recommended Version: V1 baseline, kept around later for comparison.

### Approach B: Historical Return Bootstrapping (Income‑Oriented)

- Description: generate paths by resampling historical total returns and dividends for each asset and portfolio.  
- Advantages: more realistic distribution shape; preserves some income behavior; easy to explain.  
- Disadvantages: assumes “future ~ past”; limited regime modeling.  
- Data Requirements: clean historical price/dividend series; enough data length for robust resampling.  
- Computational Requirements: low–medium.  
- Implementation Difficulty: low–medium.  
- Recommended Version: V1 (optional mode), V2 (primary engine in many cases).

### Approach C: Block / Regime‑Based Bootstrapping

- Description: resample blocks of returns or condition on macro regimes to get more realistic sequence structure.  
- Advantages: captures volatility clustering and sequence risk better; improves stress realism.  
- Disadvantages: more complex design, regime definition risk.  
- Data Requirements: long histories, regime classification inputs.  
- Computational Requirements: medium.  
- Implementation Difficulty: medium–high.  
- Recommended Version: V2–V3.

### Approach D: Factor‑Driven Scenario Simulation

- Description: simulate economic factors (e.g., growth, inflation, rates) and map them to asset returns via factor models.  
- Recommended Version: V3–V4 and Long‑Term Research due to complexity.

## 8. Proposed DividendLab Methodology (V1)

For V1, DividendLab will implement a dual‑mode Monte Carlo engine:

- Mode 1 (default): GBM‑based parametric simulation with constant drift/vol and covariance.  
- Mode 2 (optional): historical bootstrapping of returns and dividends for more realistic income behavior.

### Description

We simulate many paths of portfolio value and income over the user’s horizon, using either GBM or bootstrapped returns, coupled with the portfolio construction and rebalancing rules defined in `portfolio_theory.md`. 

### Inputs

- Per‑asset expected return and volatility (from historical data or estimates).  
- Covariance matrix of asset returns.  
- Historical return and dividend series (for bootstrapping mode).  
- Initial portfolio composition and weights.  
- Contribution and withdrawal schedules.  
- Simulation settings: number of paths, time step, horizon.

### Outputs

- Simulated paths of portfolio value and income.  
- Distributional statistics: quantiles, confidence intervals, downside probabilities, drawdown stats.  
- Summary metrics aligned with validation framework (e.g., calibration metrics, Sharpe distribution).

### Workflow

1. Load portfolio, asset parameters, schedules, and simulation settings.  
2. For each simulation path:  
   - Initialize portfolio value and weights.  
   - For each time step:  
     - Draw asset returns (GBM or bootstrapping) with appropriate covariance.  
     - Apply dividends, contributions, withdrawals, and rebalancing rules.  
     - Record portfolio value and income.  
3. Aggregate across paths and compute statistics.  
4. Expose results to the Portfolio Engine and user interface.

### Dependencies

- Portfolio theory definitions (weights, rebalancing, constraints). 
- Data pipelines for historical prices and dividends.  
- Validation and stress-testing modules for evaluation.

### Assumptions

- Stationary drift/vol/covariance over the forecast horizon in GBM mode.  
- Historical data is representative enough for bootstrapping mode.  
- Dividends are modeled via yield and growth assumptions plus cut scenarios that may be simplified in V1.

### Risks

- Miscalibrated parameters leading to misleading forecasts.  
- Over‑reliance on past data in non‑stationary markets.  
- Users misinterpreting confidence intervals as guarantees.

### Reason for Selection

This dual‑mode methodology balances transparency, implementability, and realism for V1, while leaving room for more sophisticated models in later versions.

## 10. Edge Cases

We explicitly document situations where Monte Carlo output may be misleading or fail. 

Examples:

- **Market Crashes and Regime Shifts**  
  - Description: sudden large events not well captured by normal or historical sampling.  
  - Impact: underestimation of tail risk, drawdowns, and dividend cuts.  
  - Mitigation: conservative parameter choices, stress-testing module, scenario overlays.  
  - Residual Risk: extreme events always possible.

- **Dividend Suspensions and Cuts**  
  - Description: income drops not fully captured by simple yield/growth models.  
  - Impact: income forecasts too optimistic.  
  - Mitigation: explicit cut scenarios, sector‑based risk adjustments, stress testing.  
  - Residual Risk: company‑specific surprises.

- **Missing or Noisy Data**  
  - Description: short histories, illiquid assets.  
  - Impact: unstable parameter estimates, unreliable projections.  
  - Mitigation: minimum data requirements, asset exclusion rules, aggregation.  

- **Negative Interest Rates / Unusual Macro Regimes**  
  - Description: environments far from historical norms.  
  - Impact: poor bootstrapping applicability.  
  - Mitigation: manual stress scenarios and conservative assumptions.

## 11. Validation Strategy

We explain how the Monte Carlo methodology will be tested and calibrated. 

### Validation Goals

- Check that simulated distributions are consistent with historical data where appropriate.  
- Verify calibration of quantiles and confidence intervals (e.g., empirical coverage rates).  
- Ensure that portfolio behavior under simulation aligns with conceptual expectations from portfolio theory. 

### Metrics

- Distribution calibration metrics (e.g., coverage error of specified intervals).  
- MAE/RMSE for aggregated statistics (e.g., mean returns vs historical).  
- Diagnostics for tail behavior and drawdowns.

### Backtesting Approach

- Use rolling windows and walk‑forward testing: estimate parameters on a past window, simulate forward, compare to actual outcomes.  
- Use historical simulation as a benchmark: compare Monte Carlo distribution to realized distribution over multiple periods.

### Benchmark Models

- Simple historical simulation (no parameterization).  
- Alternative parameter sets (e.g., different vol estimators) as comparison.

### Success / Failure Criteria

- Success: simulated distributions reasonably match empirical properties and produce conservative, honest risk estimates.  
- Failure: systematic underestimation of downside risk, poor calibration, or pathological behavior in stress scenarios.

Detailed metric definitions and protocols are further documented in `model_validation.md`; this section focuses on how Monte Carlo ties into that framework.

## 12. Limitations

We explicitly document weaknesses of the V1 Monte Carlo methodology. 

- **Parametric Normality Assumptions (GBM Mode)**  
  - Lognormal prices and normal returns understate fat tails and extreme events.

- **Stationarity Assumption**  
  - Drift and volatility are assumed constant; real markets exhibit regime shifts and volatility clustering.

- **Historical Dependence (Bootstrapping Mode)**  
  - Future may not resemble past; structural changes can invalidate historical patterns.

- **Simplified Dividend Modeling**  
  - Dividend cuts, suspensions, and recoveries are not fully captured; income risk can be underestimated.

- **Ignoring Detailed Frictions in V1**  
  - Taxes, transaction costs, and liquidity constraints are not fully modeled; simulated wealth may be optimistic.

These limitations must be communicated clearly in user‑facing narratives and internal docs to avoid overconfidence.
